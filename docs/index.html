Abstract

Learning to follow clinical guidelines from latent decision flows.

Clinical practice guidelines encode evidence-based recommendations as conditional decision flows. These flows provide a natural source of process supervision for language models, but two challenges complicate their use. First, a flow that follows a guideline does not necessarily determine the correct answer for a specific patient. We call this problem the path–answer gap. Second, several valid flows can support the same clinical decision. We call this property path multiplicity. Answer rewards therefore provide no information about the decision process. Supervised training on annotated flows restricts the model to one trajectory and reduces answer accuracy, while process rewards based on one reference flow penalize other valid reasoning paths.

We introduce GUIDER, which jointly optimizes a controller and a policy to address these challenges. The controller learns from supervision derived from guidelines, generates plausible decision flows, and uses them to supervise the policy. The policy learns to generate clinical reasoning and final decisions. GUIDER accounts for path multiplicity by aggregating process rewards across several flows from the controller. It links adherence to correct outcomes by awarding process rewards only for correct answers. Joint optimization allows the supervision to evolve with the policy instead of relying on a fixed reference trajectory. Because the policy never receives an oracle decision path, GUIDER does not need access to guidelines during inference.

On MedGUIDE, GUIDER with Qwen3-14B achieves 82.5 weighted accuracy and 73.5 guideline adherence. These results exceed those of the strongest clinical reinforcement learning baseline by 3.3 and 3.1 points, respectively. With Qwen3-14B and Gemma4-12B backbones, GUIDER also outperforms CRPO, MRPO, and FaithMed on three benchmarks from outside the training domain: AMEGA-LLM, RealMedQA, and CancerGUIDE.

The challenge

A correct answer does not reveal the path taken to reach it.

01

Path–answer gap

A decision flow that follows a guideline does not necessarily determine the correct answer for a specific patient. Process validity complements outcome correctness, but cannot replace it.

02

Path multiplicity

Several clinically valid decision flows can support the same decision. A score based on one fixed reference path can penalize equally sound alternatives.

Distinct clinical decision flows can support the same answer, while no single flow necessarily specifies the complete answer.

The approach

Supervise the process while preserving an independent policy.

GUIDER jointly optimizes a decision flow controller and a clinical reasoning policy. The controller proposes several plausible flows. The policy independently generates its own flow and final answer.

1

Generate

A controller learns from supervision derived from guidelines and samples several plausible latent decision flows.

2

Aggregate

Monte Carlo aggregation weights flows by their scores and rewards agreement across valid alternatives rather than agreement with one oracle trajectory.

3

Gate

GUIDER grants process credit only when the policy produces the correct final answer, thereby linking process validity to the clinical outcome.

4

Optimize jointly

The supervision evolves with the policy, but the policy never observes controller flows or guidelines during inference.

Open-ended illustration

Reasoning without predefined answer choices.

AMEGA-LLM evaluates responses to open clinical questions. The model must identify factors specific to the patient and compose the final response directly.

01 Patient case

A 65-year-old African American man presents with six months of worsening urinary symptoms, including weak stream, frequency, nocturia, and difficulty initiating urination. He recently developed mild pelvic discomfort and occasional hematuria. He has a history of vasectomy and benign prostatic hyperplasia. His father was diagnosed with prostate cancer at age 74.

AMEGA question What are significant risk factors for the primary working diagnosis obtainable from the case?
02 Model response

Significant risk factors for the primary working diagnosis of prostate cancer include:

  1. Age over 65 years
  2. African American ethnicity
  3. Family history of prostate cancer father diagnosed at age 74
  4. Presence of hematuria
  5. Presence of pelvic pain
  6. History of vasectomy
  7. History of benign prostatic hyperplasia

The patient's age, ethnicity, and family history place him in a high-risk group for prostate cancer. The presence of hematuria and pelvic pain raises concern for a more aggressive disease course. The history of vasectomy is a known risk factor for prostate cancer. His BPH history may complicate the interpretation of prostate-related symptoms.

Proposed next step: PSA testing, digital rectal examination, and consideration of prostate MRI if PSA or DRE findings are abnormal.

What we learn

Clinical guidelines can provide rich process supervision without creating a dependency during inference.

  • Multiple sampled flows capture useful path diversity; gains largely saturate at four flows.
  • Moderately sized decision flows offer enough structure without unnecessary complexity.
  • Improvements transfer across MedGUIDE, AMEGA-LLM, RealMedQA, and CancerGUIDE.
